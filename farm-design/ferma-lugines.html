01 — Read the land

Observe & interact, before moving a single stone

The first principle of permaculture is patient observation. Below, the site's energies are mapped: where the sun travels, how cold air and water drain toward the river, where wind bites in winter, and what is already here. Every later decision answers this reading.

Sun & aspect

The land opens to the south and the river, gathering full sun — ideal for fruit, vines and aromatic herbs. North-facing tucks behind woodland stay cool and shaded.

Water & slope

A steady ~18 m fall from the NW pasture to the river. Rain runs off fast and is lost — the core opportunity is to slow, spread and sink it with swales and ponds high in the system.

What's already here

Stone farmhouse, a large barn, outbuildings and a track from the north; ~1 ha of mature mixed woodland by the river; open pasture and one tilled plot. Strong bones to build on.

The design response

Keep the buildings as the warm core. Wrap intensive growing close in; let the river edge stay wild; harvest water at the top; put the cash crops where the sun is best.

02 — The ethics

Three ethics hold the whole design together

🜨 Earth care

Build soil, hold water, widen habitat. The river edge and woodland are protected and rewilded; bare ground is always covered.

⌂ People care

A family fed year-round and a real income from the land — without backbreaking work. Zones keep daily chores close to home.

⥁ Fair share

Surplus herbs, honey, fruit and seedlings feed the local market and seed-swap. Yields are reinvested in the land first.

05 — Zone by zone

From the doorstep outward

Permaculture zones are organised by how often you visit. The kitchen garden you touch daily sits at your door; the wild river you barely manage sits at the far edge. Each zone earns its keep differently.

0
Home & structures
≈ 0.15 ha · all day

The warm, built core. The stone farmhouse stays the residence; the large barn is repurposed into a herb-drying loft, processing kitchen, root cellar and tool store; the second dwelling becomes a guest room and farm office. Every roof harvests rainwater; the barn's south face carries solar panels.

Bulk carbohydrate and the wine harvest. The old tilled plot grows the classic maize–bean–squash guild, rotated with potatoes, a small grain (wheat or rye) and a green-manure rest year. Managed woodland
≈ 1.00 ha · occasional

The existing oak, chestnut and hornbeam woodland is kept as a climate buffer and habitat, then lightly worked. Hazel and black locust are coppiced on rotation for firewood, poles and bean stakes; shiitake and oyster logs fruit in the shade; and pigs are rotated through in autumn to fatten on acorn and chestnut mast while clearing bramble.

5
Wild river edge
≈ 0.25 ha · rarely

The braided river edge is left wild on purpose. A riparian buffer of willow, alder and poplar stabilises the bank against flood and filters runoff before it reaches the water. It is the farm's wildlife corridor and beneficial-insect reservoir. One managed access point serves water, fishing and a swimming hole; coppiced willow yields basketry and biomass.

06 — Water & earthworks

Slow it, spread it, sink it

With an 18-metre fall to the river, rain currently sheets off and is gone. A moderate earthworks system catches that water high in the landscape and walks it slowly downhill — recharging soil, trees and ponds along the way. No big dam; just two ponds and a run of contour swales.

Anatomy of a swale

A level ditch dug on contour, its spoil mounded downhill into a tree-planted berm. Water pools, soaks in sideways, and lifts the water table across the whole slope.

Two signature guilds

The walnut guild

Walnut is allelopathic, so its companions are chosen to tolerate it — and to feed it back. A productive island around each tree:

  • Canopy: walnut — nuts, timber, shade
  • Ring: currant & gooseberry (shade-tolerant fruit)
  • Floor: comfrey & daffodil — mulch, mineral mining, vole deterrent
  • Edge: black locust — nitrogen + bee forage

The herb terrace polyculture

On the dry south slope, drought-loving Mediterranean herbs are interplanted so the harvest — and the bees — never stop:

  • Backbone: sage & lavender — long-lived, high-value
  • Fill: oregano, thyme, savory — dense ground cover
  • Pulse: chamomile & calendula — annual, self-seeding
  • Function: stone terraces hold heat, soil and water
08 — Animal systems

Animals do the work, and feed the land

Each animal is placed for the job it does, not just what it gives. They mow, weed, till, pollinate and fertilise — turning what would be chores into yields, and keeping fertility cycling on the farm rather than bought in by the bag.

🐓20–30 birds

Laying hens

Their job

A mobile coop rotates through the orchard, eating fallen fruit and pests, scratching in manure and clearing beds before planting.

Yields

Eggs · meat · pest control · fertility

🐝20–30 hives

Honeybees

Their job

Pollinate the whole farm while living off the herb terraces, orchard blossom and black-locust flow — a perfect match for the cash crop.

Yields

Honey · wax · propolis · pollination

🐐6–10 head

Dairy sheep / goats

Their job

Rotationally graze the silvopasture and swale berms, keeping grass down and browsing hedges — fire-break and mower in one.

Yields

Milk · cheese · meat · wool · manure

🐖2–4 · seasonal

Woodland pigs

Their job

Run through the woodland each autumn fattening on acorn and chestnut mast, clearing bramble and tilling ground for replanting.

Yields

Meat · charcuterie · land clearing

Ducks

Patrol the lower pond and garden edges, hoovering up slugs and snails — the gentlest pest control there is, safe near seedlings.

The 12 principles, at work here

Every move on this land answers a principle

01Observe & interact — a full year of reading before earthworks.
02Catch & store energy — ponds, swales, soil, firewood, solar.
03Obtain a yield — herbs, honey, fruit and milk from day one.
04Self-regulate — animals sized to the land, not the other way.
05Renewables — gravity water, sun, coppice, animal traction.
06Produce no waste — manure, scraps and prunings all cycle back.
07Patterns to details — zones follow how often you walk there.
08Integrate — hens in the orchard, pigs in the wood, bees over all.
09Small & slow — a five-year build, one family's pace.
10Value diversity — dozens of species, never a monoculture.
11Use edges — the river, terraces and forest margins are richest.
12Respond to change — phased, flexible, observed every season.
