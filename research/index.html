Invisible Ecological Infrastructure

Skilled labour, environmental knowledge, and the production of sustainability among displaced agricultural workers in Lebanon's Bekaa Valley.

Lebanon's Bekaa Valley depends structurally on displaced Syrian workers who carry out most of the daily fieldwork. We know a lot about their economic precarity and about the region's environmental stress — but very little about how the workers themselves read environmental conditions and coordinate their practice under uncertainty.

Drawing on eleven semi-structured interviews, this study examines how displaced agricultural workers build and deploy environmental knowledge in their daily work: reading subtle cues to guide irrigation, pest management and harvest timing; adjusting constantly against ecological thresholds rather than fixed schedules; circulating knowledge through channels shaped by trust and hierarchy; and framing their decisions through a moral vocabulary of responsibility and stewardship. It proposes the concept of invisible ecological infrastructure to describe how workers collectively sustain agricultural systems while remaining excluded from formal recognition and decision-making authority.

The findings are operationally relevant, not only academic: recognition of prior learning is already being implemented in Syria, and the workers' expertise documented here is exactly the kind a reconstruction-era skills system could certify and build on.

Adapted from
